Acids, bases and hydrates — what water does to a formula

Water is the thread running through all three. Dissolve a molecular compound and it may hand over a hydrogen ion, which makes it an acid. Dissolve an ionic one and it may hand over a hydroxide, which makes it a base. Or let water attach itself to an ionic solid in a fixed number and you have a hydrate — the one you will weigh in lab.

AcidsThe same substance has two names

An acid yields hydrogen ions in water. The catch students trip on is that the pure substance and its solution are named differently — HCl is a gas called hydrogen chloride until it meets water, and only then is it hydrochloric acid.

Pure substance — molecular compound named as any two nonmetals would be
Dissolved in water — acid

Both equations describe the same thing. Write water in as a reactant and the proton lands on it as hydronium, H₃O⁺; leave water out and write it over the arrow, and the proton is written bare as H⁺. Textbooks use the two interchangeably.

AcidsOxoacids — hydrogen, oxygen and one more element

An oxoacid holds hydrogen, oxygen and a third element, and takes its name from that third element. The counts of H and O vary; the naming element does not.

The pattern links straight back to the polyatomic ions from 2B: an -ate anion gives an -ic acid, and an -ite anion gives an -ous acid. Nitrate becomes nitric; nitrite becomes nitrous.

BasesYielding hydroxide instead

A base yields hydroxide ions in water. In this chapter that means a group 1 or group 2 metal joined to OH⁻ — which is just an ionic compound from 2B, read for what it releases.

HydratesYour lab: how much water came off?

A hydrate is an ionic compound carrying a fixed number of water molecules, counted with the same prefixes as molecular compounds. Heat BaCl₂·2H₂O and the water leaves. Weigh what stays and you can work out how many waters you actually drove off.

The residue is assumed to be pure anhydrous BaCl₂ — that assumption is what makes the ratio land on 2. Stop heating early and it is still holding water, so the number you calculate comes out low. A ratio under 2 means keep heating, not that the formula was wrong.

HydratesNaming them
Anhydrous versus hydrated. Copper(II) sulfate is a pale off-white powder when dry and turns deep blue as CuSO₄·5H₂O once it takes up water. For some compounds the color alone tells you which one is sitting in the dish.

Objective 8A look at organic compounds
Dr. Jalloh's note: organic chemistry is not on the exam. It is here so you know the branch exists — carbon was left out of the ionic and molecular rules precisely because it has a chemistry of its own.

What decides how a carbon compound reacts is its functional group. Swap the group and the chemistry changes, even when most of the molecule does not.

The alkanes are the plainest case — carbon and hydrogen only, each following CnH2n+2.

Formula masses here use BaCl₂ at 208.23 g/mol and H₂O at 18.015 g/mol, giving BaCl₂·2H₂O at 244.26 g/mol. The lab slider runs from complete dehydration, 4.262 g, up to the untouched 5.000 g sample.
