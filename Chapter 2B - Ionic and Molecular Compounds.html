Ionic and molecular compounds, built to the two criteria

A metal with a nonmetal gives an ionic compound; two nonmetals give a molecular one. For the ionic case every formula you write has to clear the same two tests — it must be in simplest form, and the charges must add to zero. Pick a cation and an anion and watch both tests run.

MolecularTwo nonmetals, so count them with prefixes

Order: the element further left in a period goes first, and the element further down a group goes first. Prefixes run mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa, hepta, octa, nona, deca. Drop mono on the first element only — once the first element carries a prefix, the second takes one too, even when there is just one of it.

ReferencePolyatomic ions

This table is the one you are given. The monatomic ions are not — those you read off the periodic table from the group number, and the -ide endings you learn.

Charges for the representative elements follow the group rule: groups 1, 2 and 3 give +1, +2 and +3; groups 5, 6 and 7 give −3, −2 and −1, being what each needs to reach eight. Group 4 and the noble gases sit outside this. Transition metals carry more than one charge, which is why they are named with a Roman numeral. Prefix names here keep the full vowel, so P₄O₁₀ reads tetraphosphorus decaoxide; some texts elide it to decoxide, and monoxide is elided everywhere.
